\subsection{Experimental Data Overview}

The results are organised around the three research questions introduced in Section~\ref{sec:rq}. The analysis focuses on three main comparison axes: prompt representation, planning architecture, and model behaviour across scenario configurations. In addition, an exploratory analysis is included for objective sensitivity in the shared-context natural-language setting.

The metrics and experimental conditions are defined in Section~\ref{sec:method_scenarios_models_metrics}. This chapter reports raw shelf deliveries and \gls{llm} calls together with the call-normalised delivery efficiency defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:deliveries-per-1000-calls}. Raw delivery count remains the main task-throughput measure, while the call-normalised value is used when the comparison concerns the cost of model interaction.

\subsection{Effect of Prompt Representation}
\label{sec:results_prompt_representation}

These results suggest that the evaluated models were better able to use the natural-language state description than the \gls{json} representation in this implementation. One possible explanation is that converting the simulator state into descriptive sentences made relationships among agents, targets, and constraints more explicit. Although the same state variables were available in the \gls{json} prompt, their meaning had to be inferred from field names, values, and the structure of the object. The natural-language representation therefore provided additional linguistic context for models whose pretraining is primarily based on large text corpora.

The result is also consistent with Tam \textit{et al.}~\cite{tam2024letmespeak}, who found that restricting model outputs to structured formats, including \gls{json}, could reduce performance on reasoning tasks. Their study also showed that the effect was task-dependent and that structured formats could remain competitive on classification tasks. In the present experiments, the \gls{json} condition changed both the state representation and the output constraint, so the observed difference cannot be attributed to either factor in isolation. A model trained or post-trained with greater exposure to structured data might behave differently. Moreover, \gls{json} can encode relationships through meaningful field names and hierarchy, but the results indicate that this encoding was less effective than the descriptive representation for the models and planning interface evaluated here.

\subsection{Effect of Planning Architecture}
\label{sec:results_architecture}

RQ2 compares centralized planning with shared-context per-agent planning. Because shared-context planning can call the \gls{llm} separately for multiple agents, \gls{llm} calls are partly a consequence of the architecture itself. For this reason, the architecture comparison is interpreted primarily through mean deliveries, while call-normalised efficiency is treated as a secondary cost measure.

Table~\ref{tab:central_shared_scenario} reports the scenario-level comparison. Shared-context planning produced more mean deliveries in every scenario, although it also required more \gls{llm} calls. Centralized planning therefore appears more economical in terms of calls, while shared-context planning produced higher task throughput in this dataset.

\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|}
\hline

tiny\_balanced\_1v1 & 404.35 & 2.03 & 635.72 & 3.49 \\ \hline
small\_balanced\_2v2 & 398.82 & 1.50 & 909.68 & 2.64 \\ \hline
small\_agv\_heavy\_4v2 & 524.41 & 3.62 & 1622.50 & 7.94 \\ \hline
small\_picker\_heavy\_2v4 & 588.15 & 2.11 & 1974.08 & 5.42 \\ \hline
medium\_balanced\_4v4 & 675.76 & 3.09 & 1949.80 & 5.41 \\ \hline
medium\_agv\_heavy\_6v2 & 534.39 & 3.29 & 1533.08 & 6.34 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}

\caption{Planning architecture comparison by scenario. Shared-context planning achieved higher mean deliveries in all scenarios but also required more \gls{llm} calls.}
\label{tab:central_shared_scenario}

The model-level architecture comparison is less uniform. Shared-context planning improved delivery throughput for the Llama models, Mistral, Gemma, and Qwen, but Phi4 delivered slightly more under centralized planning. This indicates that the best planning architecture may depend on both model behaviour and scenario size.

One possible explanation for the overall shared-context advantage is that each model call selects an action for only one agent. This reduces the number of simultaneous decisions that must be produced and avoids requiring the model to construct a mutually compatible joint action in a single response. Centralized planning nevertheless receives the complete shared state and, in principle, can account for dependencies among all agents directly. The results show that access to global information alone did not produce higher throughput under the centralized prompt used here; the model also had to integrate that information successfully into a joint plan.

The smaller architecture gap for several of the larger evaluated models, together with Phi4's slight centralized advantage, is compatible with the hypothesis that some models handle the additional joint-planning demand more effectively. Related work has reported stronger performance from a larger backbone in planning-based logical reasoning~\cite{zhao2023explicitplanning}, while evaluations of autonomous language-model planning have also found substantial differences between models and identified the strongest tested model as the best performer, although its absolute success rate remained limited~\cite{valmeekam2023planning}. These findings support treating planning capability as model-dependent, but they do not establish parameter count as the cause of the present architecture differences because the evaluated models also differ in model family and post-training.

\subsection{Effect of Model Choice and Scenario Complexity}
\label{sec:results_model_choice}

RQ1 asks how model size affects collaborative task performance. The results show no simple monotonic relationship between nominal model size and performance. Some smaller models performed competitively under particular settings.

In the natural-language prompt comparison, llama 3b achieved the highest mean deliveries among the models, with 10.50 mean deliveries and 5.71 deliveries per 1000 calls. Phi4 achieved the highest delivery efficiency among the natural-language model group, with 6.58 deliveries per 1000 calls, despite lower mean deliveries than llama 3b. Qwen 14b and Gemma 12b performed better under natural-language prompting than under \gls{json}, but neither showed a consistent advantage simply because of size.

Scenario complexity also affected performance. Natural-language prompts produced the strongest delivery efficiency in the tiny balanced and medium AGV-heavy scenarios, while the small picker-heavy and medium balanced scenarios were less efficient. This suggests that model performance is shaped by the interaction between model behaviour, prompt representation, planning architecture, and the collaboration demands of the scenario.

Overall, the results support a cautious interpretation of RQ1: model choice matters, but model size alone is not sufficient to predict collaborative performance. The smaller llama 3b model was often competitive or strong, while larger models such as Gemma 12b and Qwen 14b did not uniformly dominate.

The comparatively low performance of some larger models should not be interpreted as evidence that larger models are intrinsically less suitable for collaborative planning. Rather, the results show that, for some of the evaluated configurations, smaller models achieved task performance comparable to or better than that of larger models. Since smaller models generally require less memory and inference computation, they may provide a favourable performance--resource trade-off for locally deployed planners~\cite{dawane2026onddeviceslm}. Computational cost was not measured directly in this comparison, so the present result establishes competitive task performance rather than a quantified cost reduction. It nevertheless indicates that increasing nominal parameter count is not always necessary to obtain effective performance in a fixed collaborative planning setting.

\subsection{Model Adaptability to Objectives}
\label{sec:results_objective_sensitivity}

This analysis compares three objective families: reducing \gls{llm} calls, balancing shelf delivery with battery awareness, and prioritising battery management to see how different models were able to adapt to the intended objective. The comparison is limited to the shared-context natural-language setting, where the objective statement was the only difference between runs.

Table~\ref{tab:objective_shared_natural} summarises the aggregate objective-level results. The Battery + Shelf objective had slightly fewer mean calls and slightly higher delivery efficiency than the Calls objective. The Battery objective produced the highest mean deliveries and the highest mean number of calls.

To examine whether models adapted to the call-reduction objective, the analysis compares each model under the Calls objective and the Battery + Shelf objective. The difference is computed as Battery + Shelf minus Calls. Negative values for mean calls indicate that the model used fewer calls under Battery + Shelf, while positive values indicate more calls under Battery + Shelf. This comparison shows mixed behaviour: llama 3b, Mistral, Gemma, and Phi4 used fewer calls under Battery + Shelf than under Calls, while llama 1b and Qwen used more. The objective statement alone therefore did not consistently produce reduced-call behaviour across models.

Battery-related behaviour was examined using the descriptive battery-aware productivity score defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:battery-aware-productivity}. The score indicates whether charging behaviour occurred together with successful delivery performance; it is a descriptive indicator, not an optimality measure. Table~\ref{tab:battery_aware_productivity} reports this score across the three objective prompts. The Battery objective produced the highest score for llama 1b, llama 3b, and Mistral, while Battery + Shelf produced the highest score for Phi4. Calls produced the highest score for Gemma and Qwen, showing that battery-related behaviour did not align uniformly with the explicit Battery objective across all models.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The results provide three main findings. First, natural-language prompts outperformed \gls{json} prompts in both mean deliveries and deliveries per 1000 \gls{llm} calls across all scenario and model groupings. Second, shared-context planning produced higher mean deliveries than centralized planning in the scenario-level comparison, but at the cost of substantially more \gls{llm} calls. Third, model performance did not scale monotonically with nominal model size: some smaller models, particularly llama 3b, performed strongly, while larger models did not consistently dominate.

The objective-sensitivity analysis adds a more cautious observation. Models did not consistently adapt to the call-reduction objective by reducing \gls{llm} usage, and battery-aware productivity varied by model. These results are best interpreted as descriptive evidence of behavioural tendencies, not as a conclusive evaluation of objective adaptation.
